# R3 — Grounded generation and book-to-concept extraction

External-fact investigation for [#5](https://github.com/ukhirani/booktube/issues/5) (part of #1). Research only: no product code, no architecture recommendation.

**How to read this file.** Every claim carries a link to the source that owns it. Sources are primary where possible — papers, official API docs, library source, benchmark repos. Where a number comes from a secondary write-up it is labelled as such. The final section lists decision-relevant facts and stops there.

## 1. Citation-preserving generation, and how citations get verified

**The gap is real and measured.** [ALCE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14627) (Gao et al., ACL 2023) introduced a benchmark for *automatic citation evaluation* and found that even the strongest models fail to cite every statement they should — on the order of half the statements on its harder ELI5 setting — while producing fluent, plausible text. Its three citation metrics are **completeness** (did it cite everything it needed to), **consistency** (does the cited span actually entail the statement), and **grounding** (is the cited span itself trustworthy), reported alongside fluency and correctness. The separation matters: correctness and citation quality move independently.

[AttributionBench](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.886/) (Findings of ACL 2024) makes the same point from the other direction: across 12 attribution tasks, even a *fine-tuned* GPT-3.5 only reaches roughly 80% macro-F1, and the paper's core finding is that **generation ability and attribution ability are decoupled** — a model that writes well does not automatically verify well.

**What vendor citation APIs actually give you.** [Anthropic's Citations API](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/citations) takes document chunks (split on sensible boundaries such as sentences or paragraphs, or supplied as explicit character/page ranges) and requires the model to point at the chunk supporting each statement; citations are enabled all-or-none across the documents in a request. [Gemini's grounding metadata](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/generate-content/google-search) returns `groundingChunks` (the sources) plus `groundingSupports`, which map a character span of the output (`startIndex`/`endIndex`) to one or more chunk indices; the newer Interactions API inlines the same thing as `url_citation` annotations. The common denominator across providers is a **span → source** mapping, not a particular JSON shape.

**Benchmarks available for claim-level checking.** [SciFact](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14974) supplies ~1.4K scientific claims with human-written evidence rationales and Supports/Refutes labels — the standard claim-verification corpus.

## 2. Naive chunking vs structure-aware extraction

**The measured effect of chunking design.** [HOPE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.02171) ("A New HOPE: Domain-agnostic Automatic Evaluation of Text Chunking", SIGIR 2025) proposes a domain-agnostic chunking metric built from three properties — concept unity, **semantic independence between passages**, and information preservation — evaluated across seven domains. Two results are load-bearing:

- Semantic independence is what pays: moving from minimum to maximum measured independence corresponds to **+56.2% factual correctness and +21.1% answer correctness** in downstream RAG.
- The intuitive assumption does not: **maintaining concept unity within a passage had minimal impact** on system performance.
- Lexical proxies are useless here: BLEU-style overlap correlates poorly with RAG outcomes while HOPE correlates significantly (ρ > 0.13).

**Structure-aware (hierarchical) chunking, measured.** [HiChunk / HiCBench](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1372/) (ACL 2026; [arXiv:2509.11552](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.11552)) argues existing RAG benchmarks cannot compare chunkers because they suffer **evidence sparsity** — only a few sentences are relevant, so any chunker finds them. HiCBench annotates chunk boundaries at multiple hierarchy levels and builds evidence-dense QA. On its `T1` task with Qwen3-32B, fixed 200-token chunking reaches **74.06 evidence recall vs 81.03 for hierarchical chunking + Auto-Merge retrieval** (Fact-Cov 63.20 vs 68.12). On evidence-*sparse* sets (GutenQA, OHRBench) the same methods are within ~1 point of each other. The benchmark you use determines whether chunking looks like it matters.

**Where the book hierarchy comes from.** For digital books the hierarchy is usually already in the file: PyMuPDF's [`Document.get_toc()`](https://github.com/pymupdf/PyMuPDF/blob/main/docs/document.rst) returns the outline as `[level, title, page, dest]` triples, and EPUB ships navigation (nav/NCX). Where no outline exists, GROBID does structure recovery: its [segmentation model](https://grobid.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Principles/) labels each line into zones (title page, header, body, footnotes, bibliography) and a token-level fulltext model detects section structure, emitting [TEI XML](https://grobid.readthedocs.io/en/latest/TEI-encoding-of-results/). GROBID's own docs are candid about limits: a small amount of training data means "inconsistent structures are sometimes predicted", and XML validation failure is treated as a *signal to filter* rather than a bug — i.e. structure extraction needs a per-document quality flag.

## 3. Book → chapter → section → concept → claim → evidence

**The hierarchy has precedent.** [BookSum](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.08209) (Chang et al., 2021) is the standard multi-granularity book dataset, with aligned **paragraph-level, chapter-level and book-level** summaries — proof that the book/chapter/paragraph ladder is a workable unit system, and a ready-made eval target.

**Extraction with LLMs: survey-level.** [LLMs for Knowledge Graph Construction and Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13168) (Zhu et al., Zhejiang Univ / Ant Group joint lab) surveys what LLMs can and cannot do for entity/relation extraction and KG reasoning. A later survey line organises LLM-based KG construction as a layered pipeline — **ontology/schema → extraction → knowledge fusion** ([arXiv:2510.20345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.20345)).

**The claim-evidence link needs source tracing built in.** [Are Large Language Models Effective Knowledge Graph Constructors?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11297) (Quek et al., 2025) proposes *hierarchical* extraction across multiple levels of abstraction rather than sentence-level triplets alone, and names the four things existing approaches skip: **coreference resolution, entity/relation deduplication, source tracing for verifiability, and multi-level organisation**. Their source-tracing step associates each extracted triple with its original text span — explicitly so downstream answers can be citation-backed. For a book-to-concept pipeline this is the load-bearing detail: provenance is part of extraction, not a post-hoc pass.

**End-to-end relation extraction** can be done by generation rather than pipeline tagging: [REBEL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15217) (Huguet Cabot & Navigli, EMNLP 2023) frames relation extraction as seq2seq over text, bootstrapping a model from a distant-supervision corpus.

## 4. Hallucination measurement and groundedness evaluation

**Atomic-fact decomposition is the dominant method.**

- [FActScore](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14251) (Min et al., 2023) decomposes long-form generation into **atomic facts** and verifies each against a knowledge source; in its setting ChatGPT-style output came out at roughly **58% factuality**. It also provides an *automated* fact-checker that approximates human verification closely, so scoring can be run continuously rather than on samples. Installable as `pip install factscore`.
- [SAFE / LongFact](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.18802) (Wei et al., 2024) does the same decomposition but verifies with **Google Search**, with structured fact-by-fact output. LongFact is its benchmark (38 topics). Headline result: when the automated judge and human crowdworkers disagreed, SAFE's judgement matched the ground truth **76% of the time** — i.e. the search-augmented evaluator beat the humans it disagreed with.
- [SelfCheckGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08896) (Manakul et al., EMNLP 2023) needs **no external source at all**: sample the model N=20 times and measure inconsistency between samples. The prompt-based variant reaches 93.42 AUC-PR on non-factual sentence detection and 78.32 Pearson at passage level, beating grey-box token-probability baselines. Useful as a cheap first-pass signal for black-box APIs.
- [HaluEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11747) (Li et al., EMNLP 2023) supplies 35,000 hallucinated/normal samples (5,000 human-annotated + 30,000 generated). Two numbers worth carrying: **19.5%** of human-annotated ChatGPT responses contained hallucinations, and ChatGPT itself recognised hallucinated summaries at only **58.53%** accuracy — near chance. Supplying retrieved knowledge lifted QA hallucination recognition from **62.59 → 76.83**.

**Do not rely on the generator grading itself.** HaluEval's near-chance self-recognition and AttributionBench's generation/attribution decoupling point the same way: use a separate verifier with access to the source (or to search), and prefer a different model family for judging than for generating.

## 5. Multi-document / cross-book synthesis

- [RAPTOR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.18059) (Sarthi et al., 2024) builds a **recursive cluster-then-summarise tree** over the corpus and retrieves across tree levels. Reported gain: roughly **20% absolute on the QuALITY dataset with GPT-4-turbo** over flat chunking, and it beats long-context baselines that read everything at once.
- [Do Multi-Document Summarization Models Synthesize?](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.13844v2.pdf) finds models are **over-sensitive to input order and under-sensitive to input composition** — they reflow whatever they are given rather than checking that the combination actually supports the summary. Their prescription: generate **diverse candidate summaries, then select or abstain**.
- [Lost in the Middle](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172) (Liu et al., TACL 2024) — position matters in a U-shape: performance is best at the start and end of context and worst in the middle, where GPT-3.5-Turbo multi-document QA dropped **more than 20 points, below its closed-book 56.1%**. Retrieval volume saturates early: going from 20 to 50 retrieved documents bought ~1.5% for GPT-3.5-Turbo while substantially increasing cost.
- [Long-Context LLMs Meet RAG](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/5df5b1f121c915d8bdd00db6aac20827-Abstract-Conference.html) (Jin et al., ICLR 2025) shows the payoff for more retrieved passages is an **inverted-U**: quality rises then falls as passages accumulate, with **"hard negatives"** (plausible, wrong chunks) as the culprit — and *stronger retrievers produce harder negatives*, degrading more than BM25. **Retrieval reordering** (best passages at start and end) is a training-free fix that matters most at large retrieval sizes.

## 6. Ingestion tooling and its limits

**Parsing accuracy is a measured, crowded field.** [OmniDocBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07626) (Ouyang et al., CVPR 2025; [repo](https://github.com/opendatalab/OmniDocBench)) scores document parsing on text edit distance, table TEDS and formula CDM, averaged into an Overall. On the project's v1.6 full leaderboard: **PaddleOCR-VL-1.6 96.34, MinerU2.5-Pro 95.75, GLM-OCR 95.22, Gemini 3 Pro 92.91, GPT-5.2 86.59, Mistral OCR 85.66, MinerU-Pipeline 86.47, Marker 78.44**. Two caveats, both material: (a) the Overall is an average over nine document types, and a [secondary analysis](https://daslab.run/blog/document-parsing-benchmarks) of the same repo reports that per-type ordering is unstable — classical pipelines beat VLMs on newspapers while losing badly on handwriting; (b) the ground truth was machine pre-annotated (LayoutLMv3, PaddleOCR, UniMERNet, GPT-4o) before human correction, so the leaderboard partly rewards agreement with the pre-annotator.

**Library-by-library.**

| Tool | License (primary source) | What it does well | Hard limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| [PyMuPDF](https://github.com/pymupdf/pymupdf) | **AGPL v3**, commercial from Artifex | Fast low-level PDF access, `get_toc()` outline → hierarchy, `pymupdf4llm` Markdown export | AGPL obligations if shipped in a closed product; no OCR in the open build |
| [pdfplumber](https://github.com/jsvine/pdfplumber) | **MIT** (pdfminer.six foundation, also MIT) | Best-in-class table extraction from the text/graphics layer, visual debugging | Slow; no OCR, so **no scanned PDFs at all**; its own README compares the field |
| [Docling](https://github.com/docling-project/docling) | **MIT** ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17887)) | Layout (DocLayNet) + table structure (TableFormer), unified `DoclingDocument`, reading order, runs locally; [docling-core](https://github.com/docling-project/docling-core) ships the doc model *and* chunking APIs | Model weights carry their own licenses |
| [Unstructured `partition_pdf`](https://github.com/Unstructured-IO/unstructured/blob/main/unstructured/partition/pdf.py) | (see repo) | Strategies `fast` / `auto` / `hi_res` / `ocr_only`, layout model, optional table structure inference, connectors to downstream frameworks | `hi_res` is the only accurate mode and it is slow/model-dependent; `fast` is a text-layer pass |
| [sentence-transformers](https://www.sbert.net/docs/migration_guide.html) | Apache-2.0 | Default embedding library | The v5 line carries **breaking renames and behaviour changes** across `encode`/similarity helpers, plus new `SparseEncoder` — pin versions at ingestion time |
| [pgvector](https://pgxn.org/dist/vector/README.html) | PostgreSQL license | `vector` ≤ 2,000 dims, `halfvec` ≤ 4,000 dims; HNSW needs no training (tune `ef_search`), IVFFlat needs a build step and `probes` | Dimension ceiling, index build/probe tuning |

**Embeddings move fast.** The [MTEB leaderboard](https://huggingface.co/spaces/mteb/leaderboard) is the reference ranking, and MTEB ≥ v2.11 supports **Matryoshka models with on-the-fly `embed_dim`** ([release notes](https://docs.mteb.org/whats_new/)), so a model can be evaluated at 128/255/512/1024 dims without retraining — relevant to pgvector's 2,000-dim ceiling.

## 7. Structured-output portability across LLM vendors

JSON Schema is the de-facto interchange format, but the *supported subset* and the *failure behaviour* differ per provider.

- **OpenAI** ([Structured Outputs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/structured-outputs)) supports a JSON Schema subset under `strict: true`, and **rejects unsupported keywords at request time** rather than silently dropping them.
- **Anthropic** ([Structured outputs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs)) exposes `output_config.format: {type: "json_schema"}` plus `strict: true` on tools, both backed by grammar-constrained sampling; it explicitly documents support for *a subset* of JSON Schema, and its SDKs **transform schemas before sending** — removing unsupported constraints, adding `additionalProperties: false`, and filtering string formats. Note the migration churn: `output_format` is deprecated in favour of `output_config.format`, with beta headers required for the old path.
- **Cross-provider behaviour** is catalogued in dottxt's [providers comparison](https://docs.dottxt.ai/providers-comparison), which tests `pattern`, min/max items, `enum`, required-vs-optional fields, `anyOf`/`oneOf` and recursive references across providers — the observed behaviours fall into three camps: **hard reject**, **strip to a description**, or **silently ignore**. The same schema can therefore be valid, transformed, or fatal depending on the vendor.
- **Citation shapes differ the same way** (§1): Anthropic uses document/char offsets, Gemini uses `groundingChunks` + `groundingSupports` spans or inline `url_citation`. A vendor-neutral layer needs its own canonical span→source type.

## Implications for the V1 pipeline decision

Facts only. No architecture choice is made or implied here.

1. **"Add citations" is a verification problem, not a prompt problem.** Even top models miss citations for roughly half the statements they should support on ALCE's harder setting, and the best attribution systems top out around 80% macro-F1 (AttributionBench). Budget for an explicit citation-checking step.
2. **Chunking quality is only observable on evidence-dense tasks.** HiCBench shows a 74.06 → 81.03 evidence-recall gap between fixed-200-token and hierarchical chunking on evidence-dense QA, while on evidence-sparse benchmarks the same methods differ by ~1 point. Benchmark on a task shaped like the real one, or chunking will look free.
3. **Design target for chunking = semantic independence between passages, not concept unity.** HOPE measures a 56.2% factual-correctness swing on independence and *minimal* impact from keeping concepts together — counter to the usual "don't split a thought" heuristic.
4. **Book hierarchy is nearly free when the file has an outline** (`get_toc()`, EPUB nav) and expensive otherwise; GROBID explicitly expects structure errors and treats failed validation as a filter signal — so any extracted hierarchy needs a per-document confidence flag.
5. **Groundedness measurement is off-the-shelf, and it is a recurring cost.** FActScore (atomic facts + automated estimator, pip-installable), SAFE (search-augmented; right 76% of the time it disagreed with human annotators), SelfCheckGPT (zero-resource sampling consistency, N=20). Pick one and cost it per generated answer.
6. **Never let the generator be its own judge.** HaluEval: ChatGPT recognised hallucinated summaries at 58.53% (near chance); supplying retrieved evidence lifted QA recognition 62.59 → 76.83. Verification needs the source, and ideally a different model family.
7. **Retrieval volume, ordering and hard negatives are first-class quality levers.** More context has an inverted-U payoff (ICLR 2025) with hard negatives from *stronger* retrievers causing more damage, and position sensitivity is a U-curve (Lost in the Middle, >20-point swing, below closed-book). "Retrieve more" is not a strategy.
8. **Cross-document synthesis is the weak link.** Multi-document summarisers are over-sensitive to input order and under-sensitive to composition, and the recommended mitigation is diverse candidates + select/abstain; RAPTOR's hierarchical tree buys ~20 points on QuALITY over flat chunking.
9. **Parser choice is document-type-specific, and leaderboards hide that.** OmniDocBench Overall is a mean over nine document types (PaddleOCR-VL-1.6 leads at 96.34), with secondary analysis showing per-type orderings reverse between newspapers and handwriting; ground truth was machine pre-annotated. Test on the actual book/document mix.
10. **Two constraints to check before committing to a tool stack:** PyMuPDF is **AGPL** (commercial license available) while pdfplumber and Docling are **MIT**; and JSON Schema is not portable as-is across vendors — providers differ in supported subset and in whether they reject, strip, or ignore unsupported keywords, so a cross-vendor schema must be pinned to the intersection or transformed per vendor.
