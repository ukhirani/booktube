# R5 — Recommendation architectures beyond engagement: external research findings

- **Wayfinder ticket:** Issue #7 "Recommendation architectures beyond engagement"
- **Status:** Research only. No architecture is chosen here (separate human ticket).
- **Method:** Primary sources preferred — papers, engineering blogs, official product documentation. Every material claim carries a URL. Each section ends with explicit notes on what transfers to a small, launch-scale system versus what presumes massive behavioural data.

## 1. Hybrid stacks: candidate generation + learned ranking + re-ranking

**The canonical multi-stage pattern.** YouTube describes the classic two-stage split: a *candidate generation* network retrieves "a small subset (hundreds)" from a corpus of millions using coarse collaborative signals, then a separate *ranking* network scores candidates with "a rich set of features" against a desired objective. The paper states the two-stage design exists to make recommendations tractable from a very large corpus, and that it explicitly "enables blending candidates generated by other sources" (non-personalized/popularity, subscriptions, etc.) into the candidate pool.
— Covington, Adams, Sargin, *Deep Neural Networks for YouTube Recommendations* (RecSys 2016): https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/45530.pdf

**Ranking is multi-objective in production.** YouTube's "watch next" ranker groups objectives into two families — *engagement objectives* (clicks, degree of engagement, time spent) and *satisfaction objectives* (likes, ratings, dismissals, user survey responses) — and trains them jointly with Multi-gate Mixture-of-Experts (MMoE) because the objectives conflict; position/selection bias is handled with a "shallow tower". Live A/B results are reported separately for engagement metrics (time spent) and satisfaction metrics (survey ratings, dismissals).
— Zhao et al., *Recommending What Video to Watch Next: A Multitask Ranking System* (RecSys 2019): https://research.google/pubs/recommending-what-video-to-watch-next-a-multitask-ranking-system/ (full text PDF: https://daiwk.github.io/assets/youtube-multitask.pdf)

**Re-ranking is a distinct third stage.** Diversity/list-quality is handled *after* pointwise ranking by dedicated re-ranking components that can be dropped in behind any ranker:
- Personalized DPP re-ranking for diversity, "easily deployed as a follow-up component after any existing ranking function", deployed in a large industrial recommender; notes users differ individually in their diversity propensity — a single global diversity weight is unrealistic.
  — Wang et al., *Personalized Re-ranking for Improving Diversity in Live Recommender Systems* (2020): https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.06390
- Taobao's homepage feed re-ranks to jointly optimize accuracy + diversity over the *sequence* (bi-sequential DPP), serving "billions of items to hundreds of millions of users every day".
  — *Multi-factor Sequential Re-ranking with Perception-Aware Diversification* (MPAD): https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.12420
- The oldest standard tool: Maximal Marginal Relevance — greedy relevance-minus-redundancy re-ranking with a tunable λ ∈ [0,1].
  — Carbonell & Goldstein, *The Use of MMR, Diversity-Based Reranking* (SIGIR 1998): https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~jgc/publication/The_Use_MMR_Diversity_Based_LTMIR_1998.pdf

**Simpler architectures also scale — the stages exist for corpus size, not as dogma.** Amazon's item-to-item CF computes an expensive similar-items table *offline* and serves online lookups whose cost "scales independently of the number of customers and number of items"; it worked at Amazon's "29 million customers and several million catalog items" with subsecond latency and produced good recommendations "based on as few as two or three items".
— Linden, Smith, York, *Amazon.com Recommendations: Item-to-Item Collaborative Filtering* (IEEE Internet Computing 2003): https://www.cs.umd.edu/~samir/498/Amazon-Recommendations.pdf (DOI: https://doi.org/10.1109/MIC.2003.1167344)

**Transfer notes.**
- The *shape* (retrieve → score → re-rank/deduplicate) is documented at every scale and the re-ranking layer is explicitly designed to be bolted onto any ranker — including a simple one.
- The *volume assumptions* are not: YouTube's candidate generation was trained on "hundreds of billions of user logs everyday"; its ranker runs on one of the largest platforms on earth (https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/45530.pdf). Learned retrieval/ranking stages are only justified when there is enough interaction data to train them.

## 2. Embedding retrieval vs collaborative filtering vs knowledge graphs; where each wins

**Collaborative filtering (interaction-matrix methods).**
- *Item-item CF* wins on scalability and low-data per-user behaviour: online cost independent of catalog size; works from 2–3 user interactions (Amazon 2003, links above). This is the classic "small data, big catalog" answer.
- *Matrix factorization* wins on raw accuracy when dense explicit/implicit feedback exists: "matrix factorization models are superior to classic nearest neighbor techniques", and they incorporate implicit feedback, temporal effects and confidence — but they require a populated user×item matrix and are defeated by sparsity/cold start (https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/5197422; free PDF: https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/classes/fa17/cse291-b/reading/Recommender-Systems.pdf).
- *Simple CF beats deep learning more often than the literature implies.* A systematic reproduction of 18 neural top-N papers: only 7 were reproducible, and **6 of those 7 were often outperformed by tuned nearest-neighbour/graph baselines**; the remaining one didn't consistently beat a well-tuned linear ranking method.
  — Dacrema, Cremonesi, Jannach, *Are We Really Making Much Progress?* (RecSys 2019): https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06902

**Embedding (two-tower / neural) retrieval.**
- Wins at *large corpus + content-rich items + cold items*: two-tower models encode item content features in an item tower, train with in-batch negatives, and are deployed "to retrieve personalized suggestions from a corpus with tens of millions of videos" (YouTube NDR). Content features let the model represent items with little/no interaction history — a stated advantage over fixed-vocabulary softmax classifiers that "don't generalize well to new items".
  — Yi et al., *Sampling-Bias-Corrected Neural Modeling for Large Corpus Item Recommendations* (RecSys 2019): https://research.google/pubs/sampling-bias-corrected-neural-modeling-for-large-corpus-item-recommendations/
  — Yang et al., *Mixed Negative Sampling for Learning Two-tower Neural Networks* (WWW 2020): https://research.google/pubs/mixed-negative-sampling-for-learning-two-tower-neural-networks-in-recommendations/
- Caveats documented by the same papers: in-batch sampling bias under power-law item popularity; selection bias from feedback logs (unseen items never get feedback) — mitigated by mixing in uniform negatives; both fixes matter mainly when popularity is heavily skewed and logs are huge.
- Downstream retrieval literature confirms two-tower + in-batch negatives is the standard large-scale recipe (Cross-Batch Negative Sampling, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.15154v1.pdf).

**Knowledge-graph approaches.**
- KG-based recommendation is justified when you have rich item ontology and need (a) relief from data sparsity/cold start via side information and (b) *native explainability* — "the reasons for recommending these two movies to Bob can be known by following the relation sequences in the user-item graph".
  — Guo et al., *A Survey on Knowledge Graph-Based Recommender Systems* (IEEE TKDE 2021): https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.00911
- Known costs: building/maintaining the graph (mapping items to entities, extracting multi-hop subgraphs) — an ontology-engineering burden, and scaling GNNs over large graphs requires neighbourhood sampling (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.00387v1.pdf).
- Knowledge-*based* (constraint/dialog) recommenders are documented as avoiding cold start entirely but carrying "high setup costs" from expert knowledge engineering — used for complex/high-involvement decisions: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/big-data/articles/10.3389/fdata.2024.1304439/full

**Transfer notes.**
- **Without large-scale behavioural data**, CF family methods have little signal (they are functions of the interaction matrix), while content/embedding similarity and KG/constraint methods operate from metadata you already have. Dacrema's result is directly reassuring: tuned simple baselines are the correct *reference point*, not deep models (https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06902).
- Two-tower/negative-sampling machinery is a *volume* technology (millions of items, batches of negatives, sampling-bias corrections); nothing in the sources suggests it pays off at launch scale.
- Amazon's lesson — precompute expensive similarity offline, serve cheap lookups online — transfers directly to any scale and maps naturally onto an in-process vector/similarity index.

## 3. Diversity, novelty, exploration/exploitation; de-duplication and fatigue

**Diversity.**
- Diversity can *hurt accuracy metrics and still improve user satisfaction*: an online study with 2,100+ subjects showed topic-diversification "improves user satisfaction with recommendation lists" while being "detrimental to average accuracy"; changing as few as three list positions had high impact.
  — Ziegler, McNee, Konstan, Lausen, *Improving Recommendation Lists Through Topic Diversification* (WWW 2005): https://doi.org/10.1145/1060745.1060754 (PDF: https://files.grouplens.org/papers/ziegler-www05.pdf)
- MMR (§1) and DPP-based re-ranking (arXiv 2004.06390, deployed in production) are the two documented list-level mechanisms; the production paper's key operational finding is that **diversity propensity is personal** — infer it per user rather than one global knob.
- Standard offline diversity metrics: intra-list similarity/distance (ILD), catalog coverage (Netflix's *effective catalog size* measures how spread viewing is across the catalog — https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/2843948).

**Novelty / exploration-exploitation.**
- Contextual bandits are the documented mechanism for exploration: LinUCB on Yahoo! Front Page beat context-free bandits by 12.5% CTR on 33M events, and "the advantage [is] even greater when data gets more scarce" — i.e. exploration methods are *most* valuable in low-data regimes. The paper also provides the evaluation method: bandits can be reliably evaluated offline from logged random traffic.
  — Li et al., *A Contextual-Bandit Approach to Personalized News Article Recommendation* (WWW 2010): https://arxiv.org/abs/1002.4058
- Industry deployments of small-scale exploration: Disney Streaming's DeepLinUCB in a live recommender, beating LinUCB online (https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3543873.3587684); Expedia's self-service bandit platform AdaptEx for cold-start UX variants (https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3604915.3608870).
- Accuracy-trained sequential recommenders show documented *repetitive bias*: some items get zero exposure to new users, and a trivial post-filter that removes already-seen items substantially improves accuracy and novelty ("3R strategy").
  — Li et al., *Repetition and Exploration in Sequential Recommendation* (2023): https://staff.fnwi.uva.nl/m.derijke/wp-content/papercite-data/pdf/li-2023-repetition.pdf

**De-duplication and fatigue modelling.**
- *Fatigue is measurable and monetizable as ranking features.* Bing Now news feed: repeated recommendations were pervasive (≈11 of 15 items overlapping between consecutive visits within 2 hours); fatigue-aware features (prior views/clicks of same item, same category, temporal decay, position) added to LambdaMART gave **+15% overall / +34% for heavy users**; crucially, previously-seen items should sometimes be *demoted* and sometimes *promoted* depending on prior interaction with the repeat.
  — Hao et al., *User Fatigue in Online News Recommendation* (WWW 2016): https://liususan091219.github.io/pdf/www16_hao.pdf
- Modern learned fatigue: FRec (SIGIR 2024) models fatigue from short-window repetition similarity, with online industrial A/B gains: https://arxiv.org/html/2405.11764v2. Formal treatment with a no-duplicate hard constraint and content-similarity discount factor: *Fatigue-Aware Bandits for Dependent Click Models*: https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.09733
- *Product-level de-dup, no ML required*: Instagram replaces copied content with the original in recommendations (matched via audio/visual signals), labels copies, and makes repeat aggregators ineligible for recommendations on a hard threshold (10 reposts in a rolling 30 days): https://creators.instagram.com/blog/recommendations-and-originality
- *Fatigue-as-wellbeing*: YouTube limits **repeated** recommendations of body-comparison/social-aggression content to teens — "okay to watch as a single video, but could be problematic for teens if viewed in repetition" — i.e. repetition exposure itself is treated as the harm channel: https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/safer-internet-day-2024/

**Transfer notes.**
- Rule-based diversity, de-dup (seen-items, per-author spacing, identical-content replacement) and threshold policies are *training-free* and fully available at small scale — most of the anti-doomscroll behaviours they enable come from exactly this layer.
- Learned fatigue models and bandit exploration both explicitly *help* when data is scarce (Bing features are hand-built; LinUCB's advantage grows as data shrinks) — unlike deep retrieval/ranking.
- What does require scale: reliable per-user diversity-propensity estimation (production DPP paper) and subtle long-horizon repetition-bias correction.

## 4. Multi-objective optimization and constraint-based ranking

- **Competing objectives are the documented industrial norm.** YouTube's multitask ranker exists because "multiple competing ranking objectives" (engagement vs satisfaction) conflict, solved by MMoE soft parameter sharing (https://research.google/pubs/recommending-what-video-to-watch-next-a-multitask-ranking-system/).
- **Taxonomy of objectives** (Jannach & Abdollahpouri): (i) quality (accuracy/diversity/novelty), (ii) multi-stakeholder, (iii) long- vs short-term, (iv) UI-level, (v) system-level. Technical approaches listed: **re-ranking (most common)**, constraint optimization, graph-based merging, Pareto-efficiency, integrating objectives into the loss, and bandit methods.
  — *A survey on multi-objective recommender systems* (Frontiers in Big Data 2023): https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/big-data/articles/10.3389/fdata.2023.1157899/full (also https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10309)
- **The hard part is not the algorithm, it's choosing and defending the trade-off.** The survey warns of "abstraction traps": an optimized offline metric (e.g. intra-list similarity) may not match human perception, and organizations must agree on KPIs/proxies first; field studies showing metric→outcome links are rare (same URL).
- **Constraint-based ranking examples that need no learning:** constrained linear optimization for balancing goals like long-tail promotion or stock/resource constraints with "limited loss in accuracy" (Jambor & Wang, via survey); LinkedIn's talent recommendation incorporating side constraints (e.g. candidate willingness to switch) alongside semantic match (via survey); hard constraints like "no duplicates in the recommendation sequence" (https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.09733); eligibility thresholds (Instagram aggregator rule, §3); default caps (TikTok 60-minute teen default, §7).
- **Multi-task learning at industrial scale is expensive** — MMoE deployed against hundreds of billions of daily events; gradient-based multi-objective variants (MGDRec) are research-stage on MovieLens/Amazon-scale datasets (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.06367).

**Transfer notes.** Constraint-based and re-rank-based multi-objectivity (constraints → filter/space list; weights → re-rank) is explicitly the "most common technique" in the literature and is implementable without training data; learned joint multi-objective models are a scale technology.

## 5. Content-quality and creator signals beyond engagement

- **YouTube**: recommendations use "understanding of a user's viewing behavior, likes, dislikes, subscriptions, and feedback, **including from satisfaction surveys**", plus "the **reputation and quality of a channel**" and **external evaluators** to gauge how an average viewer perceives content — i.e. survey + quality-judge signals sit alongside engagement by official design: https://www.youtube.com/howyoutubeworks/recommendations/
- **Instagram**: four concrete quality/creator signals in recommendations — a ranking input giving *smaller creators* more distribution, replacing copies with the original, labelling copies, and removing aggregators under a rolling threshold; also eligibility rules (Recommendation Guidelines) and reach-limit explanations for creators (watermarks, Account Status, appeal): https://creators.instagram.com/blog/recommendations-and-originality and https://about.instagram.com/blog/announcements/instagram-ranking-explained
- **Pinterest / Field Guide to Non-Engagement Signals** (19 peer-pressure-tested propositions from a workshop with experts across 7–8 platforms, Pinterest + UC Berkeley + Integrity Institute) — the most directly decision-relevant external document found:
  - "Ranking by predicted engagement causes significantly higher time-spent and retention compared to chronological ranking" (prop. 3.1) — engagement optimization *does* work on retention; the case against it must be made on other grounds.
  - "A positive ranking-weight on quality metrics often significantly increases long-term retention" (prop. 4.1) and "using item-level survey responses in ranking helps retention" (prop. 5.4) — quality/survey signals are *not* a business trade-off in the long run.
  - Barriers named: non-engagement signals are low-scale (need labels/surveys), and their benefits arrive slowly (they can *reduce* short-term engagement).
  — Blog: https://medium.com/pinterest-engineering/the-field-guide-to-non-engagement-signals-a4dd9089a176 ; resources page (paper link): https://inspiredinternet.org/resources/
- **Pinterest ranking stance**: prioritizes explicit "inspired actions" (e.g. saves) over raw views — "quality time over time spent"; surveys ("is this content useful/inspiring?") run by an internal guild of PhD psychologists feed model evaluation: https://business.pinterest.com/blog/a-better-internet-is-possible/ and https://medium.com/pinterest-engineering/healthier-personalization-with-surveys-65177cf9bea8

**Transfer notes.** Channel/creator-level quality signals (originality, aggregator thresholds, watermark rules, guideline eligibility) are policy-and-metadata based — no interaction volume required. Survey signals are cheap to *collect* at small scale (Pinterest samples a small group of users daily) but statistically weak per-survey; the Field Guide explicitly flags survey/label scarcity as the barrier.

## 6. Explainability: what powers "why am I seeing this"

- **Facebook "Why am I seeing this post?" (2019)** — first in-app explanation of ranking: shows (a) why the post appears (friend/page/group relation), (b) which *signal categories* most influence order (interaction frequency with people; interaction frequency by media type; popularity of posts), (c) direct controls. Research finding embedded in the launch: *"transparency into News Feed algorithms wasn't enough without corresponding controls"* — explanations ship together with controls. Facebook deliberately surfaces a handful of top signals out of "tens of thousands" to avoid overwhelming users.
  — https://about.fb.com/news/2019/03/why-am-i-seeing-this/ ; coverage of the "tens of thousands" detail: https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2019/apr/01/why-am-i-seeing-this-new-facebook-tool-to-demystify-news-feed
- **Meta system cards (2023)**: 22 published cards describing ranking systems and their predicted outcomes; "Why Am I Seeing This" expanded to Reels/Explore; signal categories published in the Transparency Center: https://about.fb.com/news/2023/06/how-ai-ranks-content-on-facebook-and-instagram/
- **TikTok "Why this video" (2022)**: per-video reasons collapsed into four understandable categories (your interactions; accounts you follow/suggested; recent content in your region; popular content in your region): https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/learn-why-a-video-is-recommended-for-you ; TikTok's regulatory description of the same feature: https://disinfocode.eu/reports/tiktok/5?chapterId=44&commitmentId=219
- **Academic framing**: Zhang & Chen's survey positions explainable recommendation as the "why" of the 5W, taxonomy = user-based, item-based, model-based, post-model explanations; explanations are argued to improve transparency, persuasiveness, trustworthiness and satisfaction: https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.11192. KG-based systems additionally yield path-style explanations ("same genre as X, which you watched") natively: https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.00911
- **Operational pattern**: every deployed example derives explanations from the *same logged signals/features used by the ranker* (interaction frequency, follows, content category, popularity, recency) — explanations are a presentation layer over an interpretable signal stack, not a separate model. Counterfactual/post-hoc XAI (SHAP-style) is not what any of these products ship.

**Transfer notes.** Signal-category explanations + controls are entirely feasible without large data and are the documented minimum viable transparency; they constrain the architecture *up front* (the system must be able to say which signal dominated — implying the ranking is decomposable into attributable signals).

## 7. Documented cases of optimizing for wellbeing, intent or satisfaction instead of raw engagement — and the metrics used

| Case | What was changed | Metrics used | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| YouTube multitask ranking | Jointly optimize engagement *and* satisfaction heads instead of one engagement score | Engagement: time spent on recommended videos. Satisfaction: user survey ratings, dismissals, likes | https://research.google/pubs/recommending-what-video-to-watch-next-a-multitask-ranking-system/ |
| Twitter/X algorithm audit (preregistered) | Compared engagement-ranked vs reverse-chronological vs *stated-preference* ranked timelines | Item-level "do you want to see this?" surveys; partisanship & out-group-animosity of content; readers' feelings about out/in-group. Engagement ranking amplified out-group hostile content (+0.24 SD) and users did *not* prefer its political picks; stated-preference ranking reduced anger/partisanship but risked reinforcing pro-attitudinal content | Milli et al., *PNAS Nexus* 2025: https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/4/3/pgaf062/8052060 (preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16941) |
| Pinterest non-engagement program | Add quality + survey signals to ranking | Long-term retention lifts (props. 4.1, 5.4); *negative* finding: "broad measures such as general well-being, life satisfaction, polarization … usually do not show statistically significant changes due to ranking even with relatively large and long-running experiments" (prop. 6.3) → measure *specific* content-level self-reports instead (prop. 6.1) | https://medium.com/pinterest-engineering/the-field-guide-to-non-engagement-signals-a4dd9089a176 |
| Pinterest Home Feed Relevance Survey | Rate queued pins pre-launch & post-launch; used to veto/repair models | Per-pin relevance ratings + follow-up "why not relevant"; detected and fixed a live relevance regression | https://medium.com/pinterest-engineering/healthier-personalization-with-surveys-65177cf9bea8 |
| Instagram stopping point | "You're All Caught Up" mid-feed marker (2018) — explicit end-of-new-content signal | Product designed against compulsive scrolling (time-well-spent); note: Instagram later appended "Suggested Posts" *after* the caught-up point, i.e. the stopping point became a floor for continued engagement | https://techcrunch.com/2018/05/21/scroll-responsibly/ ; https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-rolls-out-suggested-posts-at-the-end-of-your-following-feed-to-al/583825/ |
| YouTube teen wellbeing defaults | Take-a-Break (default 60-min trigger), Bedtime reminders, autoplay off by default for teens; limits on *repeated* recommendations of harmful-to-teens content | Feature defaults by age cohort; repetition-exposure as the harm metric | https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/safer-internet-day-2024/ ; https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/teen-mental-health-tools-2025/ |
| TikTok teen screen-time | Default 60-minute daily limit for all <18 accounts; passcode to extend; weekly screen-time recap | Tool-uptake metrics (prior prompt → +234% use of screen-time tools); weekly usage recaps; limit chosen with Digital Wellness Lab, explicitly noting no consensus "right" screen time | https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/new-features-for-teens-and-families-on-tiktok-us |
| Netflix (the counterargument) | Uses medium-term engagement/retention as the *target*, with engagement justified as a retention proxy | A/B over 2–6 months on hours streamed + churn; retention is the true north; effective catalog size (ECS) tracked as a diversity/health metric | https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/2843948 |

**Transfer notes.**
- The satisfaction/survey metrics in YouTube's ranker are *sparse, explicit signals* — they exist because the platform can afford large survey panels; at small scale the same *kinds* of signals (dismissals, "not interested", saves, explicit ratings) exist but with far less statistical power.
- Pinterest's prop. 6.3 is a warning that applies universally: broad wellbeing metrics will not move from ranking changes at *any* experiment size a small platform can run; choose narrow, content-level or behavioural proxies (repeat-exposure rate, dismiss rate, catch-up-point exits, survey relevance).
- Product-level wellbeing features (stopping points, limits, recaps) are training-free and directly implementable regardless of data volume.

## Implications for the recommendation architecture decision

*Facts only — the choice of architecture belongs to the human decision ticket.*

1. **The industry-standard stack is retrieve → rank → re-rank, but its justification is corpus size.** YouTube's two-stage split exists to cut millions of candidates to hundreds (https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/45530.pdf); re-ranking layers (MMR/DPP) are explicitly designed to bolt onto *any* ranker, even a simple one (https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.06390). A three-*step* pipeline does not imply three *learned* models.
2. **Learned retrieval/ranking presumes massive interaction logs; the published systems run on hundreds of billions of events/day** (YouTube multitask paper) or 2–6-month A/B tests measured against retention (Netflix, https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/2843948). Without large-scale behavioural data, two-tower/negative-sampling machinery and MMoE-style joint models have nothing to train on; Amazon's pattern — offline precompute, cheap online lookup — is the documented small-data-scalable alternative (https://www.cs.umd.edu/~samir/498/Amazon-Recommendations.pdf).
3. **Simple, tuned baselines frequently match or beat neural recommenders.** 6 of 7 reproducible neural top-N methods lost to tuned kNN/graph baselines (https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06902). Any sophisticated candidate must be justified against popularity / item-kNN / content-similarity baselines, not against itself.
4. **Anti-engagement-collapse behaviours live mostly in the rule/constraint/re-rank layer, which needs no training data**: diversity re-ranking (MMR/DPP), seen-item de-dup, per-author spacing, identical-content replacement, aggregator/eligibility thresholds (Instagram, https://creators.instagram.com/blog/recommendations-and-originality), fatigue demotion features (https://liususan091219.github.io/pdf/www16_hao.pdf), and no-duplicate constraints (https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.09733). This is also where multi-objective "constraint-based ranking" is cheapest — re-ranking/constraints are the survey's most common technique (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/big-data/articles/10.3389/fdata.2023.1157899/full).
5. **"Why am I seeing this" is a presentation layer over attributable signals — and the products' own research says explanations must ship with controls** (Facebook 2019, https://about.fb.com/news/2019/03/why-am-i-seeing-this/). This is an up-front architectural constraint: the ranking must remain decomposable into signal categories a user can be shown (TikTok's four categories: https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/learn-why-a-video-is-recommended-for-you).
6. **The engagement-vs-satisfaction split is achievable as literally two metric families**: YouTube separates engagement objectives (time spent) from satisfaction objectives (surveys, dismissals, likes) and optimizes both (https://research.google/pubs/recommending-what-video-to-watch-next-a-multitask-ranking-system/). At small scale the same split can exist as *metrics and hand-tuned weights* rather than learned heads.
7. **Survey/stated-preference signals are the documented alternative to engagement — and they correlate with retention**: quality metrics and item-level survey responses increase long-term retention per the Field Guide's cross-platform propositions (https://medium.com/pinterest-engineering/the-field-guide-to-non-engagement-signals-a4dd9089a176); the Twitter audit shows engagement-ranked feeds fail users' stated preferences on political content (https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/4/3/pgaf062/8052060). But these signals are *low-volume by nature* — the Field Guide names limited scale as the primary barrier — so at launch they are feasible only as a thin, explicitly noisy channel (Pinterest samples small daily panels: https://medium.com/pinterest-engineering/healthier-personalization-with-surveys-65177cf9bea8).
8. **Broad wellbeing metrics will not detect anything at small scale**: "general well-being, life satisfaction, polarization … usually do not show statistically significant changes due to ranking even with relatively large and long-running experiments" (Field Guide prop. 6.3). Whatever welfare intent BookTube encodes must be proxied by narrow, directly observable measures (repeat-exposure counts, dismiss/hide rate, stopping-point exits, per-surface relevance surveys) — the metrics used in the documented teen-safety and stopping-point features (YouTube repetition limits: https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/safer-internet-day-2024/; TikTok tool-uptake: https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/new-features-for-teens-and-families-on-tiktok-us).
9. **Exploration is cheapest exactly when data is scarce**: contextual bandits' advantage over context-free methods grows as data shrinks, and they are evaluable offline from logged random traffic (https://arxiv.org/abs/1002.4058) — unlike every deep ranking component above. A small randomized slice is a viable exploration mechanism from day one.
10. **What genuinely cannot be assessed without large behavioural data**: learned fatigue models' subtle effects, per-user diversity-propensity estimation, multi-month retention effects of ranking changes, and any claim that a ranking choice changes wellbeing — all of the positive causal claims in §7 rest on platforms with millions of users running long experiments; a small platform can only adopt their *mechanisms* (defaults, limits, constraints, explanations) and measure narrow proxies.
